Why first-year SIPs stop, and a passbook built to keep them going

Groww's first-time mutual fund investors are most likely to stop a SIP in their first few months. I built a discovery engine to find out why from 1.6 million public posts, interviewed eight investors, and designed a concept that goes after the real cause.

The question

Plenty of people start a SIP, and many of them stop within the first year. I wanted to know what separates the investors who keep going from the ones who stop, and which of those reasons a product can actually fix.

The measure I worked towards: the share of first-year SIPs still active, or easy to restart, at month 12.

How I found out

A handful of anecdotes can support any story, so I built a discovery engine that reads what investors say in public: Reddit, YouTube comments, Play Store and App Store reviews, TradingQ&A and X. Every stage keeps its count, so nothing is dropped quietly.

An AI model first judged whether each post was relevant, then pulled structured facts from each story, every fact tied to the exact words it came from. A second, date-aware read confirmed that someone had actually stopped before they were counted as a stopper, which makes those counts a floor, not an estimate.

I checked the model against a stronger model on 300 items and against my own labels. Where agreement stayed low, as it did for open-ended "unmet need" themes, those themes are treated as colour, not evidence.

What the evidence says

Losses start the doubt. Mechanics end the SIP.

Among first-year investors thinking about stopping, 55% describe negative returns. But among those who actually stopped, the most common routes are payment problems and app friction. Continuers see losses too: 21% of them mention negative returns, the same share as stoppers.

It happens early

49% of first-time stoppers who say when they stopped did so within three months, and 25% in months 7 to 12. The first months mix set-up problems, like a failed first debit or confusion in the app, with early losses. From month four, seeing losses or flat returns dominates.

Unanswered support is a warning sign

Of first-year stoppers who describe what they tried in the app before stopping, 40% had contacted support without getting a resolution.

Flexibility exists but isn't used

Continuers hold a long-term view (71% of their reasons). Almost nobody describes pausing or reducing a SIP instead of stopping it, even though apps offer both.

Where a product can help

I compared how often each problem area shows up in stop stories versus continue stories. The two areas that separate them on every source are payments and "where is my money".

Public posts over-represent loud failures; people who quietly let a SIP lapse rarely post about it. So I interviewed eight first-time investors to test what the data couldn't show.

  • 4 of the 5 who had stopped did so after a life event: a job loss, a job switch, a stipend ending, a family emergency.
  • 6 of 8 followed their SIP mainly through bank SMS, which shows money leaving but not what it became.
  • 4 of 8 confused their own return with the fund's.

What I built

Your bank's SMS shows the money leaving. SIP Passbook shows what it became.

UPI moves money in seconds; a SIP takes days. While an instalment is in between, the investor sees a debit SMS and nothing else. SIP Passbook gives each SIP one screen that works like a bank passbook: every instalment shows each step, from bank debit to order placed to units allotted, with its source and the time it was last confirmed.

One AI feature, Sensecheck, explains any line in plain words, using only verified facts and approved help content. It declines investment-advice questions with a fixed answer.

The rules I set before building

  • No investment advice, predictions or "you should continue" nudges.
  • No fear, urgency, streaks or guilt. Leaving is never made harder, and every option carries equal visual weight.
  • A step that hasn't happened is never shown as done. Missing data says "not confirmed yet", with the time.
  • It's labelled a supplementary view; the consolidated account statement stays the official record.

Tested before it ships

Sensecheck can only go live by passing an evaluation gate. On 20 hand-labelled questions it gave acceptable answers to 18, meeting the 90% bar, with no critical errors. An optional personal layer, a note to your future self and life markers, can be switched on or off for user tests.

The concept started broader, as a "SIP locker" with receipts, memories and a day-one promise, and I cut it back to the passbook.

Limits and next steps

Public data can't size quiet drift, eight interviews are a small sample, and the passbook runs on synthetic data. The next test is whether seeing each instalment's status reduces "where is my money" contacts and stops in the first quarter for real users.
